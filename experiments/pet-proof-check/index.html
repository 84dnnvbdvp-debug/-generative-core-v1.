Retired experiment

Pet Proof Check

This Vire Public Lab experiment tested whether a small interactive decision tool added useful protection when someone claims to have found a missing pet. The first prototype was published at commit b7d9eca1df92e7462e3a632818426c197c6c8177.

Result: the cheaper baseline tied it

A plain competent checklist reproduced every decision-relevant result in the tested cases. The interactive version filtered which instructions appeared, but there is not yet evidence that the branching improves correctness, safety, comprehension, or reunion outcomes enough to justify the extra code and maintenance.

Rather than expand the prototype to defend the original idea, the active product experiment is retired. Git history preserves the interactive version and this page preserves the correction.

The surviving checklist

  1. Do not share a one-time verification code or pay an unverified claimant. Urgency, caller ID, a payment request, or a convincing image is not proof that someone has your pet.
  2. If they claim to be a vet, shelter, animal control agency, police department, or other institution: find that institution's official contact information independently and ask whether your pet is physically in its care. If confirmed, use the institution's official release and payment path. Real shelters may have legitimate reclaim, boarding, vaccination, or treatment fees.
  3. If they are an individual finder: ask for a pet detail that was not in your public post. Prefer independent possession verification through a vet/shelter microchip scan that you confirm with the facility, or a safe public handoff at a vet, shelter, or police station. A low-tech finder or unmicrochipped pet can still use this path.
  4. Treat photos and videos as supporting evidence only. They can be real, stolen, altered, or generated; none of those possibilities is settled merely by looking at the file.
  5. If there are threats, ransom demands, suspected theft, or an unsafe handoff: preserve the messages and contact local law enforcement and the platform where the contact occurred. Real possession does not make the interaction safe or legitimate.

What was tested

The hostile comparison included a spoofed institution asking for payment, a legitimate shelter with pre-release fees, an individual finder, a verification-code request, image/video-only “proof,” a low-tech finder, an unmicrochipped pet, an emergency-vet story, an inconsistent story, and threat/ransom conditions. The checklist and interactive version reached the same consequential next actions in those cases.

The interactive prototype also mostly represented pre-verification unresolved or unsafe states rather than a full verified/disproven lifecycle. That limitation was not repaired because the baseline comparison did not justify adding more machinery.

Reopen only with new evidence

This experiment can be reconsidered if actual users show that conditional interaction materially improves decisions over the checklist, if a new scam mechanism cannot be routed cleanly by the short baseline, or if a real case exposes harm or ambiguity the baseline misses. Public visibility does not mean anyone has validated or requested this product.
